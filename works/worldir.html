01Question

How does a suggestion become a scene?

A world model can suggest a convincing room. A 3D scene needs things the image never promised: measurable positions, persistent structure, objects that can be selected, moved and replaced.

WORLDIR is my attempt to work in the layer between the two. It treats world-model output as evidence, candidates and hypotheses — never as final geometry — and turns it into decisions that a 3D pipeline can inspect and execute.

The project is a research prototype in two versions. Version 1 turns structured scene descriptions into editable Unreal Engine actors. Version 2, in development, separates what was observed from what was inferred and compiles only what has been accepted.

02Evidence

One mesh. Nine placements. Not nine guesses.

The first bounded result is deliberately small: a corridor floor. The reconstruction supports six floor placements and leaves gaps. A production scene needs nine.

Instead of quietly filling the gaps, WORLDIR records the three missing placements as design-inferred: they are allowed into the production scene, they are labelled, and they carry no geometric authority. All nine reuse a single mesh.

03Replacement

From a reference image to an editable object.

The reconstruction gives me a place to work. A generated mesh gives me an object to edit. The real problem is deciding whether the two agree well enough to be used together.

  1. 01

    Prepare the reference

    Isolate the chair from the room image so generation has one focused subject.

  2. 02

    Inspect the mesh

    Read the generated candidate as geometry, from four sides, part by part.

  3. 03

    Fit and diagnose

    Place it against the source reconstruction; check position, orientation, scale and depth.

  4. 04

    Review and accept

    Keep the disagreement on record, then make an explicit human decision.

  5. 05

    Return to the scene

    The chair goes back into the room as a separate, selectable object.

Step 01 — ReferenceIsolate what needs to be rebuilt.

The chair in the room image is only 193 × 228 pixels. I rebuild it as a standalone appearance reference — a description of upholstery, silhouette and proportion. It is an input for generation, not a measurement of hidden geometry or of where the chair stands.

Step 02 — CandidateGenerate an object, then read it as geometry.

An external generative-3D tool supplies the mesh. I inspect it from four native axes and colour its parts. A model’s “+Z view” is not automatically the front of the chair, so the labels keep that uncertainty.

Step 03 — FitAppearance is a starting point. Placement needs evidence.

The fragmented source chair holds the spatial evidence; the generated mesh offers a complete surface. Projection and depth checks reveal what a clean preview hides: an outline can look right and still sit at the wrong depth.

Step 04 — DecisionA useful fit still needs someone to say yes.

The first review kept the chair as a candidate, because local mismatches remained. I then reviewed it in the scene and accepted it for this example under a more permissive production tolerance. The acceptance changes the asset’s state; it does not erase the recorded differences.

Step 05 — ResultReplace the object. Keep the context.

Result

One accepted replacement, with its limitations kept. This does not establish collision readiness, metric accuracy or an automated whole-scene service.

04Architecture

Give the scene an editable shell.

Replacing furniture is only part of a usable scene. The room also needs walls that can be inspected and edited independently of the noisy reconstruction.

I compare the room’s footprint at low, middle and high slices to separate a recurring boundary from local clutter. An opening stays a dashed line rather than becoming a measured wall.

Boundary

The reference-photo view is not independently calibrated. This is a source-space consistency check; watertightness, collision and physical dimensions need separate validation.

05Assembly

Bring the replacements back into one room.

A single object can look convincing alone. Several have to work together: furniture against walls, equipment on work surfaces, everything against the surrounding layout. In these views generated geometry and retained reconstruction fragments coexist — the mixed state of a scene that is still being rebuilt.

Boundary

The 90.7% figure belongs to the chair test alone. These views do not establish the same fit for every asset, nor an end-to-end pipeline.

Spatial uncertainty

Single-view depth is ambiguous.

A plausible view leaves scale, occlusion and camera pose unresolved. Decision: keep unsupported space explicit.

Representation gap

Reconstruction is not production geometry.

An early mesh of about 529k vertices and 939k faces was fragmented. Decision: inspect geometry, not the preview.

Placement authority

An asset is not a scene decision.

A generated chair can look right and still disagree with source depth. Decision: fit and validate before acceptance.
